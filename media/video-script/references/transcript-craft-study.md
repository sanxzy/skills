# Transcript craft study

This study compares the 16 transcript-derived examples from 15 videos included in this skill. It informs writing decisions; it does not establish factual truth for a new script.

Use the [bundled collection](../examples/transcript-derived/stories-worth-studying.md) for complete timelines and paired source records. The [inventory](../examples/transcript-derived/stories-worth-studying.inventory.json) identifies those local files. All examples discussed below are bundled; no original extraction files or prior conversation are required.

## Coverage and limitations

The collection includes Indonesian investigations, geographic journeys, daily-life and tradition documentaries, and English wildlife narration. Rockefeller has separate Indonesian and English versions from the same video. Read the matching companion for each example's edits and factual limitations.

This is a transcript study, not a frame-by-frame video review. Timestamp spacing, music/sound cues, and visual-dependent wording suggest editorial opportunities, but do not establish the actual shot, performance, or edit. Automatic-caption paragraph boundaries frequently split sentences and overlap in time. They are extraction artifacts to repair, not a model for new scripts.

The extraction label “Quality: high” describes caption-processing metrics, not factual accuracy or editorial quality. Names are often rendered inconsistently; figures and short words are sometimes lost; some phrases depend on unseen graphics. Apparent nonsense may be transcription damage. Clear narrative choices, however, can still be studied. No claims about medicine, wildlife, geography, current wealth, policing, or historical reform should be carried forward without independent research.

## Three useful craft families

### Indonesian long documentary and geographic narration

The investigation scripts open with a result or a contradiction, establish stakes, ask the central question, and return to earlier events. They mostly progress chronologically, with explanatory detours into the systems that allowed an outcome. The Rockefeller narration uses successive identities as an escalating spine, then connects the arrest to earlier evidence. The Ames narration turns one person's conduct into an account of institutional blind spots. The Hiraoka narration delays the decisive evidence while explaining why the investigation stalled.

Their strength is causal continuity and concrete supporting detail. Their weakness is accumulation: multiple names, exact dates, official procedures, and repeated explanations of the same institutional failure can slow progression. The Hiraoka transcript includes graphic details that are unnecessary for a broad-age script. Claimed procedural reforms and motives need their own evidence, even when they make an elegant ending.

The travel scripts begin with landscape or a notable contrast, supply scale/history/economy, then move through destinations. Spain works as a national-to-regional tour. Kazakhstan uses affordability and contrasting cities as organizing interests. Iceland first explains how natural conditions become economic resources, then shifts into a long sightseeing sequence. Tristan develops isolation through terrain, ecology, settlement, public services, and livelihoods rather than remaining a list of attractions.

These scripts readily suggest maps, wide shots, architecture details, seasonal contrasts, and everyday activity. Geographic transitions keep the route legible. Repeated “not far from there,” interchangeable praise, and recurring village descriptions can make later stops feel replaceable. A scenic tour must stay aligned with the title's promise if the opening promises an economic explanation.

### Mechanism-led educational narration

The bundled [rain example](../examples/clouds-have-water-why-doesnt-it-always-rain.srt) gives a concrete observation, explains mechanisms in dependency order, introduces conditions and alternatives, and follows the consequences after rainfall reaches the ground. The [pattern workshop](../examples/from-flat-facts-to-stories-worth-watching.md) provides short hypothetical exercises for early answers, causal transitions, comparisons, and Indonesian clarity.

Use familiar objects and direct explanations when they help the viewer understand. Introduce an unfamiliar term alongside the action it describes. Questions, humor, and analogies are optional; they should clarify the mechanism rather than delay the answer or imply unsupported science. Visual-dependent phrases such as “this year” need the actual date in narration or a specified available graphic.

### English wildlife documentary narration

The wildlife scripts often begin with a place or an individual facing a concrete task. Facts enter when they explain what the viewer is watching: finding food, feeding young, escaping, conserving energy, or accessing a resource. A recurring individual or family gives large-scale environmental information a humanly comprehensible thread without needing a stream of rhetorical questions.

The jungle script repeatedly returns to a young orangutan while following seasonal changes, other species, and wider environmental threats. The Yunnan script follows hornbill provisioning, elephants, and other adaptations, then connects species through shared resources. The wildlife compilation carries individual contests mostly through sparse narration and long action intervals. The snake script uses a comparison framework and periodically revisits its criteria, distinguishing biological capability from practical exposure and outcomes.

Their strength is selective narration: it supplies context and mechanism while leaving documented action room. Their risks are anthropomorphism, dramatic animal labels, incomparable rankings, unsupported species-wide conclusions, and claims that sound exact but lack visible evidence. An action-heavy transcript's low word density cannot be transplanted into a new explanation without corresponding footage.

## Findings by dimension

| Dimension | Observed strength | Risk in imitation | Working rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hooks | Outcome, contrast, familiar question, concrete task | Vague danger, stacked questions, unverified superlatives | Establish subject and evidence promptly |
| Structure | Chronology, geographic route, mechanism, seasonal thread | Unrelated history or attraction lists | Choose the spine that answers the title |
| Pacing | Dense explanation followed by action or orientation | Caption length mistaken for editorial timing | Time original spoken blocks and useful visuals |
| Density | Details attached to an action or mechanism | Lists of figures and names | Give each fact meaning before adding another |
| Storytelling | Documented choices, obstacles, changed conditions | Invented motives or omniscient scenes | Narrate only what evidence supports |
| Rhythm | Short emphasis amid fuller explanations | Endless fragments or long procedural clauses | Vary sentence function, read aloud |
| Facts | Numbers and specific examples create scale | Precision and confident delivery mistaken for accuracy | Verify scope and calculations independently |
| Audiovisual use | Spatial descriptions, action, music intervals | Unavailable footage and unexplained “this” | Match wording to producible images |
| Endings | Return to original individual, question, or consequence | Repeated moral lessons and grand claims | Pay off the promise once |

## Applying the study

Borrow functions, not signatures: an opening contrast, a causal link, a useful exception, an observed task, a clear geographic move, or a supported callback. Do not copy catchphrases, ornamental rhythms, unsupported claims, or extraction timestamps.

The user-selected standard takes precedence over the corpus. Favor plain explanation, controlled emphasis, realistic timing, and independently sourced facts even when a reference uses ornate drama or a much shorter format. The bundled original examples demonstrate this adaptation.
