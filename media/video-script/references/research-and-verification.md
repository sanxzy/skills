# Research and verification

Use this reference before drafting and when checking a revised script. The output of research is a usable, verified claim ledger, not a stack of links.

## Research from the promise

Translate the brief into questions that would let the viewer understand the subject. For an explanation, find what happens, how it works, why it matters, and where the simple explanation stops. For history, establish chronology, participants, evidence, and what changed. For a place, connect terrain, access, livelihoods, settlement, and present conditions. For a comparison, define the comparison measure before ranking anything.

Search broadly enough to discover the story, then narrowly enough to verify it. Useful query patterns include an exact event plus an official institution; a scientific mechanism plus a review paper; a statistic plus its dataset name; or a proposed quotation plus the speaker and original interview. Search in local languages when official records or local reporting offer better evidence. Translation must preserve uncertainty, legal distinctions, and units.

Read the relevant page, table, paper section, or document with enough surrounding context to check its qualifications. A title or short search-result snippet suggesting support is insufficient. Accessible indexed page text, an institutional mirror, or a retrieved document passage can support a claim when its original source and context are identifiable; a teaser or summary cannot substitute for that passage. If direct opening fails, record the failure and exactly which retrieved text was inspected, without claiming a complete page read. If no adequate passage is accessible, find another reliable source or omit the claim.

## Choose evidence by claim type

| Claim | Strong starting point | Check before use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current population, price, budget, ranking | Official statistical office, current dataset, regulator | Reference period, geography, definition, revisions |
| Scientific mechanism | Relevant scientific institution, original research, review | Consensus versus hypothesis, experimental conditions |
| Health effect | Clinical guidance, systematic review, strong original study | Association versus causation, population, uncertainty |
| Legal outcome | Judgment, court release, official case record | Allegation versus charge versus conviction; appeals |
| Historical event | Archive, primary document, scholarly history | Contemporary versus retrospective account; disputed dates |
| A named person's action or quotation | Original statement, document, full interview, reliable reporting | Exact wording, context, attribution, independent evidence |
| Geographic superlative | Defined geographic dataset, relevant official authority | What is measured; inhabited versus permanent; category |
| Wildlife behavior | Published observation, biological authority, documented footage | Species, typical versus exceptional behavior, location |

Official sources can still be mistaken, outdated, partisan, or imprecise. Authority is a reason to investigate, not a guarantee. A promotional tourism page can establish what an attraction advertises; it is weaker evidence for an objective world record. A first-hand account establishes what the speaker reports, not necessarily the full cause of an event.

## Claim ledger

Before writing, record material claims at a granularity that makes them checkable:

| ID | Proposed claim | Evidence and locator | Scope/caveat | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C01 | Proposed factual sentence | S01, named section/table/page | Time, place, definition | Verified / qualify / omit |
| C02 | Proposed explanatory relationship | S02, relevant passage | Mechanism or inference? | Verified / qualify / omit |

Do not combine a supported date, an unsupported motive, and a speculative consequence into one “verified” row. Split them. A named event in a hook needs the same verification as one in the body.

Verify important, surprising, disputed, sensitive, or central claims through independent evidence where possible. One authoritative source can suffice for a settled definition. Two articles derived from one press release count as one evidence chain. Do not require a fixed source count at the expense of relevance.

## Seven checks on wording

1. **Identity:** Does the source discuss this species, location, person, or institution?
2. **Quantity:** Are numerator, denominator, units, currency, and reference period preserved?
3. **Time:** Is the claim historical, current, projected, or a scenario?
4. **Strength:** Does “associated with” become “causes”? Does “can” become “always”?
5. **Scale:** Is a local observation being presented as global or typical?
6. **Attribution:** Are claims by a witness or researcher being narrated as established fact?
7. **Completeness:** Does removing a qualification change the meaning?

Preserve distinctions such as wealth versus cash, gross versus net income, median versus mean, toxicity versus real-world fatalities, installed capacity versus energy produced, and elapsed time versus active work. These distinctions can become the story's central insight.

## Calculations and comparisons

Store input values, direct sources, formula, unit conversions, date, and rounding. Recalculate rather than copying a striking comparison from a transcript. Explain the assumption in narration if it materially changes interpretation. Comparing a one-time stock of wealth to an annual flow of wages requires saying what the comparison means; it does not demonstrate what would actually happen if assets were sold.

Do not generate pseudo-precision. If underlying data are approximate, round narration to a meaningful scale. Avoid unnecessary statistics when a verified concrete explanation does more work.

## Causality and uncertainty

Chronological order does not establish causality. To say a policy caused an outcome, find evidence of the mechanism and consider other explanations. If evidence establishes only that changes occurred together, say that. If experts disagree, describe the actual disputed point rather than adding a vague “some people disagree.”

Useful language includes “the records show,” “the team reported,” “the study found an association,” “one explanation is,” and “the evidence does not establish why.” Use these only when they identify a real evidentiary distinction. Do not weaken an established fact with needless hedging.

## Last factual pass

Check the script sentence by sentence after stylistic edits. Look especially for adjectives and connectors added for drama: “first,” “only,” “largest,” “never,” “therefore,” “because,” “deliberately,” and “inevitably.” They introduce factual claims. Recheck dates and current figures, resolve contradictions, remove unused sources, and map all material claims to final narration timecodes.

Research stops when the selected story's claims are supported, meaningful uncertainty is handled, and remaining searches would not change the script. It does not stop because the link count looks impressive.
