# Audiovisual writing

Use while shaping beats into timed narration. The main script is a clean narration timeline; optional production notes belong in the sources file.

## Give the editor something concrete

Each substantive beat should suggest an image, demonstration, diagram, map, record, comparison, or documented action. You need not prescribe a shot for every sentence. Choose descriptions that make the point visible: a channel carrying water downhill, the changing position of a boundary on a map, a document revealing a date, or droplets forming on a glass.

Abstract statements need a representable mechanism. “The economy changed” is weak alone. A supported explanation of what was produced, how it moved, and who bought it gives the editor usable choices. A graphic can communicate scale, but it should not make uncertain data appear exact.

## Complement the image

Narration should supply what the image cannot: context, cause, scale, timing, or significance. Do not describe every visible movement if the action already reads clearly. Conversely, do not leave the audience to infer the scientific mechanism from scenic footage.

Sparse wildlife narration works because the footage carries an observable task and outcome. A new explanatory script without that footage needs more spoken explanation. Transcript runtime is not a universal word budget.

Deictic phrases such as “this,” “here,” “the person you see,” and “in the year shown” depend on a specific visual. Use them only when that visual is specified and available. Prefer complete narration such as “In 1908...” to an unexplained “In this year...” unless the user has deliberately chosen a visual-dependent format.

## Evidence and footage are different

Do not narrate a hypothetical animal, individual, experiment, or historical scene as something the camera recorded. If illustrating a general process, use general language or an explicitly hypothetical situation. Stock footage of a city does not prove a particular event happened there at that time.

Keep proposed shots conditional when availability is unknown. Describe their function in production notes: “diagram showing the two paths” is enough; do not imply the project already contains such a diagram. Label archival material with accurate time/place context where that distinction affects interpretation. Research permission to use footage is separate from evidence for a claim; do not promise clearance without checking it.

## Silence has a job

A gap can let the viewer locate a place, compare images, watch a result, hear an ambient sound, or absorb a change. State its purpose in the sources file's optional beat notes. A music-only block is useful only when music itself carries a real transition or sequence.

Avoid long gaps around text that needs only a few seconds. Do not transplant a two-minute chase interval into an explanation merely because a wildlife transcript contains one. For a new documentary with no footage brief, use modest visual space and enough narration to carry the story.

## Editorial boundaries

Break narration at complete thoughts and changes of focus. Avoid splitting a sentence just to imitate a caption export. A block can span several shots; a shot does not require a new narration block. Keep important qualifiers attached to their claims rather than separated by a long visual interval.

An on-screen title is optional. If it is spoken, include it as narration. If it is only displayed, describe it in production notes and leave suitable timeline space. Avoid ambiguous unspoken directives embedded among words a narrator should read.

The timeline should remain intelligible with a simple voice recording and relevant pictures. Detailed shot planning, asset production, voice generation, and rendering are separate work unless requested.
