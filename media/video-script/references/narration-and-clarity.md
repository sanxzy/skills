# Narration and clarity

Use when drafting and during the spoken edit. Across-age clarity means low assumed background knowledge, precise explanations, and respect for the audience. It does not mean removing meaningful complexity.

For a serious Indonesian documentary voice that develops through events, obstacles, and consequences, use [documentary-storytelling.md](documentary-storytelling.md). Clear factual writing should still feel like a story being explained, with reasons to keep listening. Transcript punctuation may need repair even when the underlying narrative style is a useful reference.

## Write for one hearing

The viewer cannot scan backward as easily as a reader. Keep the subject and verb close. Put the action before a long qualification. Make pronouns unmistakable when multiple people, places, or mechanisms are present. Split a sentence when it contains a new actor, a separate causal link, or several qualifications.

Prefer “Water enters the soil through small openings” to “The infiltration of precipitation into porous subsurface material occurs.” Both can describe the same process; the first lets the viewer picture the action.

Use short sentences for a change or emphasis and medium sentences for explanation. Constant fragments make ordinary facts sound like emergencies. Constant long sentences make the listener hold too much in memory. Rhythm comes from changing the job of sentences, not arbitrarily alternating lengths.

## Vocabulary and technical terms

Introduce the action first or explain a term in the same breath: “Water soaks into the ground. This is infiltration.” Afterward, use the term only when it helps. Avoid several unfamiliar words in one block.

Do not replace a precise concept with a misleading simplification. Clouds are not vapor made visible; they contain droplets or ice. An association is not a proven cause. A physical process does not have intentions. If a simpler phrase loses a material distinction, explain the distinction instead.

Numbers should have a purpose. Say what is counted, compared with what, and when if relevant. Round for speech when precision is unnecessary. Write abbreviations as a narrator should read them, or record pronunciation in production notes. A compact digit string can require several spoken words; timing must account for them.

For an unfamiliar measurement, establish a concrete picture before giving the quantity. “Annual rainfall is twelve and a half meters” can be mistaken for flood depth. A clearly hypothetical open container, keeping all the rain for a year, makes the quantity understandable; then distinguish that picture from water flowing or soaking into the actual ground. Keep detailed measurement periods in the evidence record when they do not change the story, while avoiding wording that presents an older average as a current measurement. A definition that only repeats the unfamiliar term does not explain it.

## Figurative language as a local tool

Use an analogy when it clarifies a difficult relationship. State the real mechanism alongside it and stop using the analogy when it stops helping. Check what false assumptions the analogy might introduce.

A “factory” analogy can help explain a sequence of operations. It can also imply separate management, intentional behavior, or machinery where none exists. A single concrete explanation may be clearer. Avoid extending one comparison across an entire script simply because it made a good opening.

Use a question when it reflects a real problem in understanding. Answer it promptly or explain why the answer needs more steps. Avoid chaining “but why,” “what happens next,” and “is it possible” at each transition. Declarative transitions often work better.

Hyperbole, personification, and dramatic one-liners can distort facts. Replace “nature refuses to let anyone survive” with the actual constraint: low temperatures, limited food, exposed terrain, or distance from help. Genuine difficulty remains interesting without an invented adversary.

## Tone and language

Match the requested language and register. Indonesian conversational narration may use forms such as “kita,” “jadi,” or “bayangkan”; it need not borrow every slang term, meme, or catchphrase from a reference creator. Clear international English need not sound academic or promotional.

Avoid cultural examples the audience must decode unless they explain more than they distract. Humor should come from a recognizable situation, not belittling a community, victim, animal, or person. An educational explanation should not drift into medical advice or assert an unsupported intervention.

For grim subjects, prefer accurate and proportionate description. A general-audience investigation can explain evidence and legal outcomes without enumerating injuries. Accessibility includes emotional and conceptual suitability, not only simple vocabulary.

## Information density

High density means each sentence earns its place. It does not mean every sentence needs a new number. A clear explanation of one important fact is useful information. A necessary qualification is useful information. Repeating “this place is isolated” with three metaphors is not.

Group a fact with its meaning before introducing another. A useful editing question is: after this block, what can the viewer understand that they could not understand before it? If the answer is only “the narrator sounds impressed,” rewrite or remove it.

## Spoken edit

Read without the source notes. Mark:

- breathless clauses and awkward consonant clusters;
- pronouns whose referents require rereading;
- repeated opening words and sentence shapes;
- lists that overload working memory;
- jargon explained after rather than before use;
- vague qualifiers that hide missing evidence;
- emotional emphasis unsupported by the content.

Then read with a neutral voice. If the script depends entirely on dramatic delivery to remain interesting, improve the underlying progression. Recheck factual meaning after every simplification.
